Domains, DNS + hosting

Own your domain. Know where it points.

A lot of businesses do not actually control their own domain name — the bloke who built the site five years ago does, and he stopped answering his phone in 2022. Get it back in your name, and know exactly what it is pointing at.

What gets sorted

The plumbing behind your website and email. Invisible when it works, and a genuine emergency when it does not.

Registering and transferring

Getting the right .com.au or .au name, or moving an existing one to an account you actually control. Registered in the business name, not a contractor's.

Getting the keys back

If someone else holds your domain, there is a documented process to recover it. Usually recoverable — the earlier you start, the easier it goes.

Renewals that do not lapse

Renewal dates, a card that has not expired, and notices going to an address someone still reads. Expired domains get bought fast and cost a fortune back.

DNS pointed where it should be

Website to the host, mail to the mail provider, nothing left over from a service you stopped using three years ago. Written down so it can be undone.

Hosting and SSL

Somewhere sensible for the site to live, the padlock working, and automatic renewal on so the certificate does not lapse and scare customers off.

Written down for next time

Registrar, host, records, renewal dates and who has access — in one document, so you are never again guessing where the site is hosted.

Signs something is wrong back there

If any of these sound familiar, the setup behind your site needs a look.

Nobody knows who hosts the site

If you cannot say who to ring when the site goes down, you do not have a supplier — you have a hope.

Your email lands in spam

Almost always missing or wrong mail records on the domain. Fixable, and worth fixing — you are losing quotes.

See business email setup

"Not secure" in the address bar

An expired or missing certificate. Customers see a warning before they see your business, and most of them leave.

A renewal invoice you do not recognise

Could be legitimate, could be one of the fake domain renewal letters that go around. Worth checking who you actually pay before paying it.

The sensible order

  1. 01

    Find out what exists

    Who the domain is registered to, when it renews, where it points and what is hosting the site right now.

  2. 02

    Get ownership right first

    Before changing anything, make sure the domain is in the business name with access you control. Everything else depends on this.

  3. 03

    Record it, then change it

    Current settings saved first. Then changes made one at a time, so anything that breaks is obvious and reversible.

  4. 04

    Check it from outside

    Site loads, padlock is there, mail sends and arrives. Checked from a device that is not yours.

Do not click yes on a DNS prompt

The most common way small businesses take their own site down is accepting a change a provider suggested, without knowing what it replaced.

  • Save a copy of the current records before touching anything
  • Changing nameservers moves everything at once, mail included
  • Changes can take hours to spread — do not panic and change it back
  • Keep registrar and hosting logins in a password manager, not a notepad
  • Turn on two-factor for the domain account, it is the master key
